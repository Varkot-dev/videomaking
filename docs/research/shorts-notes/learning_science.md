## 1. Mayer's Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning: which principles matter most for a short narrated animation, and the redundancy principle versus burned-in word-by-word captions

### Takeaway
Best supported and most relevant for a short narrated animation: coherence (cut everything non-essential), signaling (cue where to look), modality (explain with speech, not paragraphs of on-screen text) and temporal/spatial contiguity; independent meta-analyses confirm these but with smaller effects than Mayer's own lab medians. The redundancy principle is the weakest and most contested of the set: verbatim on-screen duplication of narration tends to hurt or do nothing when animation is present and pace is system-controlled (exactly the Shorts case), while a few on-screen key words help, and captions help second-language viewers. No study was found that tests karaoke-style word-by-word captions specifically.

### Cited Findings

Coherence
- Mayer (2021, Multimedia Learning 3rd ed.) reports a median effect size of d = 0.86 for coherence (exclude extraneous words, pictures, sounds), positive in 18 of 19 tests; other editions/reviews give 0.70 or 0.86 with 23 of 23. Numbers differ by edition. — [Mayer, research-based principles handout (UNH mirror)](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf); [Chartered College summary of Mayer](https://my.chartered.college/impact_article/how-multimedia-can-improve-learning-and-instruction/)
- An independent meta-analysis exists specifically on the seductive-details (coherence) effect: Sundararajan & Adesope (2020), "Keep it Coherent: A Meta-Analysis of the Seductive Details Effect", Educational Psychology Review 32(3), 707-734. Effect size not retrieved this session (see Gaps). — [WSU repository record](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Keep-it-Coherent-A-Meta-Analysis-of/99900601155501842)
- A separate meta-analysis of Mayer's corpus found the largest significant effects for removing seductive details, modality, personalization, the multimedia principle, sentence-level coherence and self-explanation. — [Zendy record, J. Computer Assisted Learning 10.1111/jcal.12197](https://zendy.io/title/10.1111/jcal.12197)

Signaling
- Mayer (2021): median d = 0.69, positive in 15 of 16 tests. — [Mayer handout (UNH mirror)](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)
- Independent meta-analysis, Schneider, Beege, Nebel & Rey (2018), Educational Research Review 23, 1-24: 103 studies, 12,201 participants; retention g+ = 0.53, transfer g+ = 0.33 (figures taken from a secondary summary, check against the paper). — [ACU summary of signalling research](https://inspire.acu.edu.au/articles/signalling); [paper DOI](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/S10648-018-9456-4)
- Second independent meta-analysis, Alpizar, Adesope & Wong (2020): overall d = 0.38 from 29 experimental studies, 2,726 participants. So independent estimates (0.33 to 0.53) are clearly smaller than Mayer's 0.69 median. — [ACU summary](https://inspire.acu.edu.au/articles/signalling)

Modality
- A review of 76 studies found a median d = 0.65 favouring spoken over printed words alongside graphics; Mayer's earlier work reported 1.17. Effect sizes for Mayer's principles are noted to have boundary conditions, for example being stronger for low- than high-knowledge learners. — [Chartered College summary](https://my.chartered.college/impact_article/how-multimedia-can-improve-learning-and-instruction/)
- Boundary condition from L2 research: Lee & Mayer (2018) found that for second-language learners a video + text group outscored the others (contrary to the modality effect) and video + narration + text outscored video + narration (contrary to the redundancy effect). — [Fostering learning from instructional video in a second language](https://pure.uos.ac.kr/en/publications/fostering-learning-from-instructional-video-in-a-second-language/)

Redundancy and captions (contested)
- Mayer's own redundancy estimate collapsed between editions: a 2017-era table lists d = 0.87, while Mayer (2021) reports a median of only 0.10 with 8 of 12 tests positive, and rewords the principle to "people do not learn better when printed text is added to graphics and narration" (i.e. from "it hurts" to "it does not help"). — [Chartered College summary](https://my.chartered.college/impact_article/how-multimedia-can-improve-learning-and-instruction/); [Mayer handout (UNH mirror)](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)
- Adesope & Nesbit (2012), "Verbal redundancy in multimedia learning environments: A meta-analysis", J. Educational Psychology 104(1), 250-263 (dissertation version: 48 effect sizes from 32 reports): the redundancy effect depended on learners' prior knowledge, the pacing of the materials and whether animation or diagrams were included; a few on-screen words summarising the key points of the narration was associated with improved learning. — [WSU repository record](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Verbal-Redundancy-in-Multimedia-Learning-Environments/99900601157101842); [SFU dissertation](https://summit.sfu.ca/_flysystem/fedora/sfu_migrate/9947/etd5852.pdf)
- Multimedia research is summarised as showing that the same words as spoken and written text accompanying graphical information hinders learning (the redundancy effect). — [Erasmus University repository paper](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/95290/REPUB_95290_AAM.pdf)
- The redundancy effect for subtitles interacts with prior knowledge: one study found a significant interaction between subtitles, labels and prior knowledge on learning performance. — [HKU repository paper](https://hub.hku.hk/bitstream/10722/308041/1/content.pdf)
- The popular claim "video captions benefit everyone" is disputed by learning-science commentators on redundancy grounds (blog-level source, not peer reviewed). — [Learning and the Brain blog](https://www.learningandthebrain.com/blog/video-captions-benefit-everyone-an-investigation/)

Segmenting
- Rey et al. (2019), "A Meta-analysis of the Segmenting Effect", Educational Psychology Review 31, 389-419: 56 investigations, 88 pairwise comparisons; a significant small-to-medium segmenting effect on retention and transfer; high-prior-knowledge learners gained more on retention. — [MPI publication record](https://is.mpg.de/publications/rey2018segmenting)

Personalization, voice, embodiment, instructor/drawing
- Mayer, Fiorella & Stull (2020), "Five ways to increase the effectiveness of instructional video", ETR&D 68(3), 837-852, reviews five ways to improve instructional video and one way not to; summaries name dynamic drawing (graphics drawn as the instructor explains), instructor gaze guidance, and prompting generative activity during the video (helps lower-prior-knowledge learners most). A 2016 study cited there found the drawing benefit disappeared when the hand was not shown. — [ECR Hub record](https://ecrhub.org/publications/five-ways-increase-effectiveness-instructional-video); [University of Sydney summary](https://educational-innovation.sydney.edu.au/teaching@sydney/five-ways-to-make-online-videos-better-for-student-learning/); [paper DOI](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/S11423-020-09749-6)
- Personalization (conversational style) is among the largest significant effects in the independent meta-analysis of Mayer's corpus. — [Zendy record](https://zendy.io/title/10.1111/jcal.12197)

### Inferences
- Ranking for a 30-180 s narrated animation, by strength of independent evidence and fit to the format: (1) coherence, (2) signaling, (3) temporal contiguity/narration-visual sync and modality, (4) personalization (cheap to apply in the script), (5) segmenting and pre-training (matter less inside a single very short video, because a Short is effectively already one segment; they matter for how a topic is split across videos).
- Word-by-word burned-in captions are the conditions under which redundancy is most likely to cost learning: full verbatim text, animation present, system-paced, fast, and the caption pulls the eyes away from the animation at the moment the animation carries the meaning. The evidence-consistent compromise is: no verbatim captions over a meaning-bearing animation; instead put 1-4 key terms or the equation on screen next to the thing they describe (that is signaling plus spatial contiguity, and is the "key words" exception in Adesope & Nesbit).
- Counter-pressures that are real but not learning effects: muted autoplay, accessibility for deaf/hard-of-hearing viewers, and non-native speakers (where the evidence actually reverses in favour of text). A defensible design is a platform closed-caption track (viewer-controlled) rather than burned-in karaoke text, or captions placed so they do not compete during the key visual transformation.
- Mayer's medians come largely from his own lab, short lab lessons, immediate tests and novice college students; independent meta-analyses consistently give smaller numbers. Report independent estimates where they exist.

### Gaps
- Could not retrieve the full Mayer (2021) table (host blocked), so effect sizes for spatial contiguity, temporal contiguity, segmenting, pre-training, personalization, voice, embodiment and image principles are not sourced here. Unverified recall of Mayer's published medians: spatial contiguity about 1.1, temporal contiguity about 1.2, segmenting about 0.7 to 0.8, pre-training about 0.75 to 0.85, personalization about 0.8 to 1.0, voice about 0.7 to 0.8, embodiment about 0.4 to 0.6. Verify before use.
- Sundararajan & Adesope (2020) seductive-details effect size not retrieved (unverified recall: overall roughly g = -0.33).
- Adesope & Nesbit (2012) pooled g not retrieved (unverified recall: spoken+written beat spoken-only overall by a small margin, roughly g = 0.15, with the advantage vanishing when images/animation were present or the presentation was system-paced).
- No study found that directly tests TikTok-style word-by-word animated captions against no captions on a learning outcome. This is a genuine hole in the literature as far as this search could tell.
- Schroeder & Cenkci (2018) spatial-contiguity meta-analysis and any temporal-contiguity meta-analysis (Ginns 2006) were not searched.

## 2. Video length and engagement, microlearning, and whether TikTok/Shorts/Reels produce learning or only the illusion of learning

### Takeaway
The "under 6 minutes" rule comes from one observational MOOC study that measured watch time, not learning; there is no consensus optimal length. Direct evidence on Shorts-style learning is thin, recent, small-sample and conflicting: some small RCTs show short-form matching or beating long-form on immediate recall, while the best-placed study (Otto 2025, Computers & Education) found short videos produced more surface learning and lower quiz scores than the same content as text. Fluency reliably inflates confidence without improving learning.

### Cited Findings
- Guo, Kim & Rubin (2014, ACM Learning@Scale): 6.9 million video-watching sessions across four edX courses; engagement measured as watch time and whether students attempted the post-video problem; engagement falls off sharply after about six minutes, so the authors recommend segments under six minutes. Authors acknowledge the difficulty of measuring "true" engagement without direct observation and limits to generalizability from historical data. — [Paper PDF (MIT CSAIL)](https://up.csail.mit.edu/other-pubs/las2014-pguo-engagement.pdf?_blank); [UBC summary](https://blogs.ubc.ca/videolearninghub/?p=569)
- A Research in Learning Technology article describes Guo et al. as showing video length to be the strongest predictor of engagement, but states the literature has no broad consensus on ideal length; another study using similar analytics found the highest engagement for videos under 10 minutes. — [Research in Learning Technology](https://journal.alt.ac.uk/index.php/rlt/article/download/2680/2917)
- Otto (2025), "Should educators be concerned? The impact of short videos on rational thinking and learning: A comparative analysis", Computers & Education 234, 105330: online experiment, 123 participants (mean age about 25), four groups (short TikTok-style learning videos versus the same content as text). Short-video learners reported a higher situational surface-learning approach and scored lower on the quiz than text learners. The author's dopamine explanation is speculation; he says short videos are not inherently unsuitable but should be designed differently, for example slower. — [Document record](https://www.commbox.org/document/should-educators-be-concerned-the-impact-of-short-videos-on-rational-thinking-and-learning-a-comparative-analysis); [press coverage](https://www.galaxus.ch/en/page/short-videos-encourage-shallow-thinking-40485)
- Contradicting evidence: "The Reel Deal" (2025, ACM): 62 university students randomly assigned to LLM-generated reels versus a long-form video; the reels group scored higher on a 6-item recall/comprehension quiz and finished faster. Small sample, immediate test only. — [arXiv 2509.05962](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.05962v1)
- A 2025 science-communication study (63 participants) is reported as finding short videos outperformed text on memory retention, contradicting Otto. Details unverified. — [Lund University student paper listing](https://lup.lub.lu.se/student-papers/record/9233401/file/9233425.pdf)
- Chiossi et al. (CHI 2023), "Short-Form Videos Degrade Our Capacity to Retain Intentions": 60-person experiment; a TikTok feed condition significantly degraded prospective-memory performance. This is about the feed context (remembering intentions), not learning from an educational clip. — [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.1145/3544548.3580778)
- Video-based microlearning studies report gains in immediate knowledge and short-term retention, with the caution that short content needs clear objectives to avoid superficiality (low-tier journal). — [Educur journal](https://journals.iarn.or.id/index.php/educur/article/download/534/388/3419)
- Fluency illusion: Carpenter et al. (2013, Psychonomic Bulletin & Review), "Appearances can be deceiving: instructor fluency increases perceptions of learning without increasing actual learning": a fluent delivery raised students' confidence but not actual learning. — [typeset.io PDF](https://typeset.io/pdf/appearances-can-be-deceiving-instructor-fluency-increases-40w2tynmko.pdf)
- A follow-up with a 31-minute lecture found the same pattern and named it an "illusion of learning" due to fluency in lecture-based learning. — [Princeton record](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/instructor-fluency-leads-to-higher-confidence-in-learning-but-not/)
- Kardas & O'Brien (2018, Psychological Science), "Easier seen than done": repeatedly watching a how-to video inflated predicted performance, but repeat viewers performed no better than single viewers. — [Chicago Booth press release](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/media-relations-and-communications/press-releases/easier-seen-than-done-watching-how-to-videos-on-youtube-gives-people-false-sense-of-expertise)
- A 2026 bibliometric review of the educational short video field exists (not read in detail). — [arXiv 2608.06932](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.06932)

### Inferences
- The honest summary for the playbook: a 30-180 s video can produce real immediate recall of one idea; there is no good evidence yet on durable retention or transfer from Shorts; and the default viewing mode (passive, fast, in a feed) predicts shallow processing plus overconfidence.
- Guo et al. supports "shorter is watched more completely", not "shorter teaches more". Completion rate is a proxy the platform rewards; it is not a learning measure. The playbook should not cite Guo as evidence that short videos teach better.
- Otto's design implication (slow down, fewer cuts, prompt active processing) and the fluency findings (smoothness inflates felt learning) point the same way: optimise for a moment of effortful thinking inside the video, not only for smoothness.
- The feed context is itself a cost (Chiossi): a viewer who swipes on immediately gets interference with no consolidation, so an end-of-video retrieval prompt or pause is worth more in this format than in a course.

### Gaps
- No direct fetch of Guo et al.; specific sub-findings are unverified recall: median engagement capped around 6 minutes regardless of length; speaking rates of 185-254 wpm had the highest engagement; Khan-style tablet drawing beat slides; informal talking-head settings beat studio recordings. Verify in the paper.
- No formal replication or critique specifically testing the six-minute threshold was found. Unverified recall: later analyses (e.g. Lagerstrom, Johanes & Ponsukcharoen 2015 at Stanford; Geri, Winer & Zaks 2017) argued students watch longer than six minutes when sessions are aggregated, and that course context matters.
- No large-sample, delayed-test, pre-registered study of learning from Shorts/TikTok/Reels versus long-form with matched content was found. Existing studies are small (n about 60-125), immediate-test, single-site.
- Otto (2025) exact effect sizes not retrieved.
- No meta-analysis of microlearning with credible quality was found in this session.

## 3. How much content fits: cognitive load, one idea per video, narration speed, pacing of visual changes

### Takeaway
Comprehension of speech holds up well to roughly 275 wpm for young adults, so narration rate is not the binding constraint; the binding constraint is how many new interacting elements must be held while watching a transient, system-paced animation. The evidence supports one idea per video and slowing visual change at the point of the key insight, but there is no empirically derived "ideas per minute" number.

### Cited Findings
- Typical speech is about 150 wpm (Peelle & Davis 2012); speech comprehension begins to decline around 275 wpm (Foulke & Sticht 1969); 2x playback of a lecture exceeds about 300 wpm. — [Murphy et al. 2023, Memory (UCLA PDF)](https://castel.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/111/2023/04/The-effect-of-video-playback-speed-on-learning-and-mind-wandering-in-younger-and-older-adults.pdf)
- Murphy, Hoover, Agadzhanyan, Kuehn & Castel (2022, Applied Cognitive Psychology), "Learning in double time": video speed had little effect on immediate or delayed comprehension up to 2x; beyond 2x, comprehension dropped. — [Paper PDF (UCLA)](https://castel.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/111/2022/03/Applied-Cognitive-Psychology-2021-Murphy-Learning-in-double-time-The-effect-of-lecture-video-speed-on-immediate-and.pdf); [ScienceDaily coverage](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2022/01/220111153637.htm)
- Boundary condition: faster speech may harm older adults while not significantly affecting younger learners. — [Murphy et al. 2023, Memory](https://castel.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/111/2023/04/The-effect-of-video-playback-speed-on-learning-and-mind-wandering-in-younger-and-older-adults.pdf)
- Segmenting (breaking a continuous presentation into learner-digestible parts) has a significant small-to-medium benefit across 56 investigations. — [Rey et al. 2019](https://is.mpg.de/publications/rey2018segmenting)
- The redundancy/overload effects depend on pacing: system-paced materials with animation are where added verbal load hurts. — [Adesope & Nesbit record](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Verbal-Redundancy-in-Multimedia-Learning-Environments/99900601157101842)
- Otto (2025) recommends slowing the pace of short educational videos to counter surface processing. — [Press coverage of Otto 2025](https://www.galaxus.ch/en/page/short-videos-encourage-shallow-thinking-40485)

### Inferences
- Word budget: at a natural 150-170 wpm, 30 s is about 75-85 words, 60 s about 150-170, 180 s about 450-510. The Murphy results imply narration up to about 200 wpm is unlikely to hurt comprehension of the words themselves for young adults, but those studies used talking lectures, not dense animation where the eyes also have work to do; conceptual animation should sit at the slow end, with silence at the reveal.
- "One idea per video" is an inference from cognitive load theory and the segmenting evidence, not a directly tested rule. Practical formulation: one target insight, at most roughly 3-4 new elements the viewer must hold simultaneously (see Gaps for the working-memory citation), everything else either pre-trained in one sentence or cut.
- Pace visual change by meaning: one visual change per narrated proposition, never an animation that is not being narrated (temporal contiguity), and hold the frame for a beat after the key transformation. High cut rates aimed at retention work against the processing time the learner needs.
- Because a Short cannot be learner-paced the way a segmented lesson is, the video must do the segmenting itself: brief pauses and clear visual "chapter" boundaries act as built-in segments. Looping/rewatch on Shorts partly substitutes for learner control, but see the Kardas & O'Brien finding that rewatching inflates confidence more than skill.

### Gaps
- No primary study found that manipulates narration wpm in a short animated explainer and measures learning. The 275 wpm threshold is from 1969 time-compressed-speech work.
- No study found quantifying an optimal rate of visual changes or cuts per minute for learning.
- Working-memory limits (Cowan 2001, about 4 chunks; Sweller's element interactivity) and the transient-information effect (Leahy & Sweller 2011; Spanjers et al.) were not searched this session; they are standard but unverified here.
- Guo et al.'s speaking-rate result (faster speakers, 185-254 wpm, were more engaging) is unverified recall and is an engagement, not learning, measure.

## 4. What increases retention and transfer: prediction prompts, worked examples, concrete-before-abstract, analogies, curiosity gap, desirable difficulties, retrieval prompts

### Takeaway
The strongest quantified levers are worked examples (g about 0.48 in maths), refutation of misconceptions (g about 0.41) and asking a question before the content; curiosity measurably improves memory, including for incidental material. Prequestion and in-video quiz effects are real in lab settings but smaller and less reliable in authentic settings.

### Cited Findings
- Prequestions: Carpenter & Toftness (2017): 85 students watched an Easter Island video in three segments; those who attempted two prequestions before each segment outperformed controls on the final test, including on six questions they had not been prequestioned on. Proposed mechanism: in video, viewers cannot skim ahead for the answers, so attention to the whole video rises. — [BPS Research Digest](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/heres-simple-way-boost-your-learning-videos-prequestion); [Learning Scientists blog](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2017/11/2-1)
- Not uniformly replicated: Toftness et al., "The Limited Effects of Prequestions on Learning from Authentic Lecture Videos" (JARMAC) argue the 2017 materials were unrepresentative; a classroom study found benefits days later that did not persist at one month. — [JARMAC paper (Royal Holloway)](https://pure.royalholloway.ac.uk/files/30437641/JARMAC_Toftness_et_al.pdf)
- A 2023 Educational Psychology Review article reviews prequestion/pretesting effects (not read in detail). — [Educational Psychology Review 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-023-09805-6)
- Curiosity: Gruber, Gelman & Ranganath (2014, Neuron): memory was better for information participants were curious about and for incidental material (unrelated faces) shown during high-curiosity states, on immediate and one-day-delayed tests; linked to anticipatory midbrain and hippocampal activity. — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4252494/)
- Worked examples: Barbieri et al. (2023, Educational Psychology Review): g = 0.48 across 181 effect sizes from 55 studies in mathematics; self-explanation prompts moderated the effect. — [Barbieri et al. 2023](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s10648-023-09745-1.pdf)
- Refutation: Schroeder & Kucera (2022, Educational Psychology Review), "Refutation Text Facilitates Learning": g = 0.41 across 44 independent comparisons (n = 3,869). A pre-registered meta-analysis by Danielson et al. (2024, Educational Psychologist) also reviews refutation-text efficacy. — [Schroeder & Kucera 2022](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s10648-021-09656-z.pdf)
- Retrieval during video: Szpunar, Khan & Schacter (2013, PNAS): interpolated memory tests reduced mind wandering (untested students reported mind wandering about 40% of the time) and improved learning of an online lecture. — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3631699)
- Mixed follow-ups: Jing, Szpunar & Schacter (2016) found mind-wandering rates did not differ between tested and restudy groups (benefit came via better integration); a 2024 paper notes results for interpolated testing at video pauses have been mixed. — [Jing et al. 2016](https://sites.harvard.edu/schacter-memory/files/2022/08/jing2016-1-1.pdf)
- Generative activity prompts during video help most for lower-prior-knowledge learners (Mayer, Fiorella & Stull 2020). — [University of Sydney summary](https://educational-innovation.sydney.edu.au/teaching@sydney/five-ways-to-make-online-videos-better-for-student-learning/)
- Self-reported fluency versus effort: feelings of having learned track fluency, not learning. — [Carpenter et al. 2013](https://typeset.io/pdf/appearances-can-be-deceiving-instructor-fluency-increases-40w2tynmko.pdf)

### Inferences
- A defensible Shorts structure from this evidence: (1) open with a question the viewer must commit a guess to (prequestion + curiosity + misconception elicitation in one move), (2) show a concrete worked instance before the general statement, (3) explicitly name and refute the tempting wrong answer, (4) end with a retrieval or "what would happen if" prompt rather than a recap.
- The curiosity-gap hook and the learning mechanism can be the same device, but only if the gap is the concept itself. A hook about something tangential is a seductive detail (coherence violation) and Gruber's "incidental benefit" does not rescue it from that cost in a 60 s video.
- Prequestion effects in Carpenter & Toftness were strongest when the viewer could not skip ahead; a system-paced Short reproduces that condition, so the format is well matched. Effect durability is doubtful (gone at one month in a classroom study).
- "Desirable difficulty" in a passive, unskippable-forward format mostly reduces to: make the viewer predict, leave a 1-2 s silence before the reveal, and ask for retrieval at the end. Difficulty that is merely perceptual (hard fonts, clutter) is not supported.

### Gaps
- Loewenstein (1994, Psychological Bulletin) information-gap theory was not retrieved; it is a theoretical account (curiosity arises from a perceived gap between what one knows and wants to know, requires some prior knowledge to open the gap). Unverified recall.
- No meta-analysis of concreteness fading was found; the search returned only theory descriptions (Bruner-derived three-stage concrete to abstract progression). Unverified recall: Fyfe, McNeil, Son & Goldstone (2014) is the standard review; Kaminski, Sloutsky & Heckler (2008, Science) is the well-known contested claim that abstract examples transfer better.
- Analogies: no source retrieved this session. Unverified recall: Alfieri, Nokes-Malach & Schunn (2013) meta-analysis of case comparison found about d = 0.50; Gick & Holyoak (1980, 1983) show spontaneous analogical transfer is poor without a hint or multiple cases.
- Pretesting/prequestion pooled effect size not retrieved (the 2023 Ed Psych Review paper likely has it).
- Retrieval practice general meta-analyses (Rowland 2014, about g = 0.50; Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan 2017, about g = 0.61) and desirable difficulties (Bjork & Bjork) not retrieved; unverified recall. The perceptual-disfluency effect (Diemand-Yauman 2011) largely failed to replicate (Rummer et al.; Xie et al. 2018 meta-analysis found about zero) is also unverified recall.
- No study found testing an end-of-video retrieval prompt specifically in a sub-3-minute social video.

## 5. Human versus synthetic voice

### Takeaway
The original voice principle (human beats machine voice) was built on studies using robotic early-2000s TTS. Studies with modern engines mostly find no reliable learning difference from a human recording; what remains is a modest cost in social presence and, in some studies, trust. Audio quality appears to matter more than voice source. The evidence base with current neural TTS and controlled visuals is still small.

### Cited Findings
- Mayer's voice principle: people learn more deeply when words are spoken in a human voice rather than a machine voice; the supporting studies were largely more than a decade old at the time of the re-tests and TTS had improved greatly. — [Craig & Schroeder 2017, Computers & Education record](https://unpaywall.org/10.1016%2Fj.compedu.2019.103605); [ERIC record](https://searchworks.jioinstitute.edu.in/articles/eric__EJ1225297)
- Craig & Schroeder (2017, Computers & Education), "Reconsidering the voice effect when learning from a virtual human": a modern computer voice was as effective as a human voice on certain social measures and outperformed it on some learning outcomes. — [ReadSpeaker summary (vendor, interested party)](https://www.readspeaker.com/blog/elearning-voice)
- Craig & Schroeder (2019, J. Educational Computing Research): instructional animation narrated by an older TTS engine, a modern TTS engine or a recorded human; modern-engine learners were in most respects not statistically different from human-voice learners on perceptions, learning outcomes or cognitive efficiency. — [ERIC record](https://searchworks.jioinstitute.edu.in/articles/eric__EJ1225297)
- Davis, Vincent & Park (2019), "Reconsidering the voice principle with non-native language speakers" exists; findings not retrieved. — [ERIC record](https://searchworks.jioinstitute.edu.in/articles/eric__EJ1225297)
- Xu et al. (2024, British J. Educational Technology), "From recorded to AI-generated instructional videos": AI-generated videos (GPT script, TTS, lip sync) matched recorded videos for learning, with higher retention and no significant transfer difference; recorded videos gave stronger social presence; satisfaction, trust and cognitive load did not differ significantly, though values were slightly lower for AI. — [Paper PDF](https://www.tiffin.edu/wp-content/uploads/Article-From-Recording-to-AI-Instructional-Video.pdf)
- Counter-evidence: a small VR study (36 participants) found AI-instructor appearance and voice significantly influenced learning performance and social presence, with best results for real-human appearance plus real-human voice. — [Beihang University record](https://research.buaa.edu.cn/en/publications/real-or-virtual-the-effects-of-appearance-voice-and-vr-for-aigc-o/)
- A 2025 study of foreign-language short videos (2x2 AI/human voice by AI/human avatar) found a significant engagement gain only when both voice and avatar were AI-generated. — [Education and Information Technologies 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-025-13654-x)
- Industry survey (TechSmith, not peer reviewed): audio quality mattered far more than whether the voice was human or AI; a high-quality AI voice was rated most professional-sounding by 92% of participants. — [Training Industry](https://trainingindustry.com/articles/artificial-intelligence/a-practical-guide-to-using-ai-voices-and-avatars-in-training/)
- A practitioner blog claims not all researchers have replicated the "modern TTS equals human" finding (non-peer-reviewed). — [okmiku blog](https://okmiku.com/blog/tts-elearning-narration-tradeoffs)

### Inferences
- For a faceless narrated animation, a high-quality neural TTS voice is unlikely to reduce learning relative to a human recording; the voice principle as originally stated should be treated as outdated for modern engines rather than refuted in general (low-quality, monotone or mis-stressed synthesis still falls under it).
- The residual risks are prosody and social presence: stress on the wrong word in a technical sentence, no natural pause before a reveal, and a voice audiences have learned to associate with low-effort content. These are quality-control items (pronunciation of terms, pause control, emphasis) more than a human-versus-machine question.
- Personalization (conversational "you" phrasing) is a script-level property and should transfer to TTS; it is the cheaper way to recover social presence.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study was found that isolates a 2024-2026 generation neural TTS voice versus a human narrator with identical animation, measuring delayed retention and transfer.
- No peer-reviewed evidence found on audience credibility penalties for recognisable "AI voice" on social platforms specifically (stigma/"AI slop" effects on trust and watch time).
- Mayer's original voice-principle effect size (unverified recall: median about d = 0.7 to 0.8 from a handful of tests, Mayer, Sabko & Mautone 2003; Atkinson, Mayer & Merrill 2005) not retrieved.
- Evidence on TTS for non-native listeners (Davis et al. 2019) not retrieved.

## 6. Misconceptions-first explanations (Derek Muller) and why clear-seeming explanations can fail

### Takeaway
Muller's PhD work found that clear, concise expository physics videos are rated highly and leave learners more confident but produce little conceptual change, while videos that first state and then refute common misconceptions are rated as more confusing, demand more mental effort and produce more learning. This converges with the independent refutation-text literature (g about 0.41) and the fluency-illusion findings.

### Cited Findings
- Muller, Bewes, Sharma & Reimann (2008), "Saying the wrong thing: improving learning with multimedia by including misconceptions", J. Computer Assisted Learning 24(2), DOI 10.1111/j.1365-2729.2007.00248.x: students were tested with mechanics conceptual inventories before and after watching multimedia; adding discussion of misconceptions promoted conceptual change across students with all levels of experience. — [Paper listing](https://perticles.blogspot.com/2008/01/muller-sharma-reimann-jcal-2008.html)
- Muller, Sharma & Reimann (2008), "Raising cognitive load with linear multimedia to promote conceptual change", Science Education 92, 278-296, DOI 10.1002/sce.20244: investigates techniques that raise useful (germane) cognitive load in linear, non-interactive multimedia, including a dialogue treatment in which a student voices alternative conceptions; viewers judged themselves similar to the student in the dialogue. — [Zendy record](https://zendy.io/title/10.1002/sce.20244); [BibBase record](https://bibbase.org/network/publication/muller-sharma-reimann-raisingcognitiveloadwithlinearmultimediatopromoteconceptualchange-2008)
- Muller & Sharma, "Tackling misconceptions in introductory physics using multimedia presentations" (conference paper, University of Sydney). — [Sydney open journals](https://openjournals.library.sydney.edu.au/IISME/article/view/6345)
- Secondary account quoting Muller: presenting common misconceptions alongside the scientific concepts increases learning by increasing the mental effort students expend while watching; watching videos does not necessarily lead to learning. — [THE Journal, 2015](https://thejournal.com/articles/2015/05/05/watching-videos-does-not-necessarily-lead-to-learning.aspx?Page=1)
- Muller's PhD (University of Sydney, 2008) is titled "Designing Effective Multimedia for Physics Education". — [Wikipedia: Derek Muller](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derek%20Muller)
- Independent support: refutation texts (state the misconception, explicitly refute it, explain the correct idea) outperform other learning conditions, g = 0.41, 44 comparisons, n = 3,869. — [Schroeder & Kucera 2022](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s10648-021-09656-z.pdf)
- Independent support for the mechanism (felt clarity is not learning): fluent instruction raises perceived learning without raising actual learning. — [Carpenter et al. 2013](https://typeset.io/pdf/appearances-can-be-deceiving-instructor-fluency-increases-40w2tynmko.pdf); [Princeton record of the 2018 follow-up](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/instructor-fluency-leads-to-higher-confidence-in-learning-but-not/)

### Inferences
- Why clear explanations fail: when a viewer already holds an intuitive model, a correct exposition is assimilated as agreeing with it; the viewer feels they "already knew that", pays less attention, and can even leave more confident in the wrong idea. Stating the misconception first forces a comparison and raises germane effort.
- This resolves an apparent conflict with coherence/cognitive-load advice: extraneous load should be cut, but effort directed at the concept itself should be raised. A misconception statement is not a seductive detail because it is about the target concept.
- For Shorts: the misconception makes an honest hook ("most people think X; here is the case that breaks it"), and it doubles as the prediction prompt. It applies to topics where a prevalent prior intuition exists (mechanics, probability, evolution, electricity); for topics with no prior model (a new definition), the mechanism has nothing to act on.
- A design caution implied by refutation research: the misconception must be explicitly flagged as wrong and immediately refuted with the reason. In a feed where many viewers drop in the first seconds, a misconception stated as fact with a delayed refutation risks teaching the misconception to those who swipe away.
- Viewer ratings and like/"so clear!" comments are a poor proxy for learning; the misconception treatment in Muller's work was the less-liked, more-effortful one.

### Gaps
- Effect sizes, sample sizes and exact treatment structure for Muller's studies could not be retrieved (host blocked, abstracts only). Unverified recall: samples of several hundred first-year physics students at Sydney; post-test gains for refutation/dialogue treatments roughly double those for exposition, with effect sizes reported around 0.8; exposition viewers described the video as "clear, concise, easy to understand" while misconception viewers reported more confusion and higher mental effort. The widely quoted figures come from Muller's own Veritasium video "Khan Academy and the Effectiveness of Science Videos" (2011), which is a self-summary, not a peer-reviewed report.
- Muller's studies are single-lab, physics-only, with immediate post-tests; no independent direct replication in video form was found in this session.
- Danielson et al. (2024) pre-registered refutation-text meta-analysis result not retrieved; whether refutation effects persist at delay, and "backfire/familiarity" risks of repeating the misconception, were not checked (unverified recall: backfire effects are now considered rare and weak, e.g. Swire-Thompson, DeGutis & Lazer 2020).
- No evidence found on misconception-first design specifically in sub-3-minute social videos.
