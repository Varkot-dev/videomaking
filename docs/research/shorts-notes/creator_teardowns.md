## Which channels are the most successful at math/science/CS concept explainers in short form?

### Takeaway
The largest audiences belong to generalist science brands (Vsauce ~25M, Kurzgesagt ~26M, Cleo Abram ~8M, 3Blue1Brown ~8.7M), but the channels whose Shorts are *native* concept explainers that an animation pipeline can imitate are 3Blue1Brown (2026 monthly puzzle Shorts, 290K to 840K views each), Mathematical Visual Proofs (~260K subs, ManimGL, wordless), Andy Math (~544K, geometry puzzles) and Fireship (~4.3M, "100 Seconds"). Several names on the candidate list are essentially long-form channels whose short-form presence I could not verify as significant (Veritasium, Mark Rober, Cleo Abram's recent uploads, MindYourDecisions).

### Inferences
- For a ManimGL-plus-narration pipeline the most relevant models are, in order: 3Blue1Brown puzzle Shorts (same toolchain, narrated, 45 to 60 s), Mathematical Visual Proofs (same toolchain, wordless), Andy Math (single geometry problem, narrated solve) and Fireship (dense narrated explainer of one concept in ~100 s).

## For each: length, first 3 seconds, structure, visual style, text, voice, music, ending, scoping

### Takeaway
The verified pattern for animation-led math Shorts is one self-contained object per video in 44 to 62 seconds (3Blue1Brown's puzzle Shorts), either a puzzle statement that ends unresolved or a single visual proof set to music with no narration (Mathematical Visual Proofs). Most second-by-second detail below could not be observed this session and is flagged accordingly.

### Inferences
All bullets here are **[prior knowledge, unverified this session]** unless they restate a cited finding.
- 3Blue1Brown puzzle Shorts: Grant's own calm, conversational voice at natural speed (not sped up); dark background ManimGL animation in the house palette (blue, teal, yellow, brown pi-creatures occasionally); opens directly on the puzzle object already on screen with a sentence of the form "Here's a puzzle..." or "Suppose you have...", no greeting or logo; on-screen text is minimal (labels and the question, no word-by-word captions burned in); light Vincent Rubinetti-style piano bed or none; ends on the explicit question, with the solution promised elsewhere. Recut Shorts from long videos keep the original narration and re-frame the animation vertically.
- Mathematical Visual Proofs: black background, statement of the identity in LaTeX at top from the first frame, shapes build and rearrange for 30 to 60 s, the final formula is written out and highlighted at the end; no voice, a single instrumental music track; authorship comes from the citation and consistent visual identity rather than from a persona.
- Fireship: opens on the name of the technology plus a one-sentence definition in the first two seconds ("X is a ..."); structure is definition, brief history/who made it, how it works, then a tiny code walk-through; dark background, logos, screenshots, memes and code flashing at roughly one visual change per second; own voice, fast and deadpan; subtle background beat; ends on a fixed sign-off ("...thanks for watching, and I will see you in the next one"). Many "100 Seconds" videos are horizontal 2 to 3 minute uploads, with vertical Shorts as a separate stream.
- Andy Math: a single diagram on a digital whiteboard filling the frame from the first frame; opens with a line like "Hey guys, this looks like a fun one" and the ask; solves by annotating the diagram live in colour, boxes the answer, then "How exciting." Own voice, relaxed, lightly dry; no music.
- Zach Star Shorts: mostly counter-intuitive probability or applied-math prompts delivered by Zach himself, with simple graphics; I am not confident of current format.
- Vsauce Shorts: Michael to camera, usually holding a physical object, starting mid-thought with a surprising claim or question; theatrical pauses; ends on a punchline or a twist, often without a sign-off.
- Kurzgesagt Shorts: the same flat vector art and professional narrator as the long videos, usually excerpts or compact single-question pieces with a full music score.
- MindYourDecisions and blackpenredpen: both are primarily long-form; Presh uses slide-style animated diagrams with voice-over and "pause the video if you'd like to try it" before the solution; bprp is face plus whiteboard with two marker colours.
- Scoping rule visible in the cited data: one object per Short. 3Blue1Brown: one puzzle statement. MVP: one identity at one value of n. Andy Math: one figure, one unknown. Fireship: one technology.

## What have these creators said about their process and what works in short form?

### Takeaway
The most directly useful statement is Grant Sanderson's (June 2026): puzzles are "perhaps the only type of math content" where he thinks short-form beats long-form, because a puzzle statement is concise and the answer should not be spoiled. Cleo Abram's guidance is to start from a key visual and cut self-contained loops; Fireship's is compression of a 30-minute topic with template-driven editing.

### Cited Findings
- Sanderson: "Puzzles are perhaps the only type of math content where I feel short-form videos are better than long-form videos. It's nice to concisely hear the statement, but you don't want the answer spoiled too soon." — [On monthly puzzles, 17 Jun 2026](https://3blue1brown.substack.com/p/on-monthly-puzzles)
- Sanderson initially made solution Shorts, viewers asked what happened to the rest, and he is moving explanations into longer videos grouped by problem-solving tactic — [On monthly puzzles](https://3blue1brown.substack.com/p/on-monthly-puzzles), [Peer review for SoME5](https://3blue1brown.substack.com/p/peer-review-for-some5)
- Sanderson is publicly wary of the medium: in the comments of his 2023 "Opium" post he wrote that the addiction is "not to TikTok in particular, but to short-form video" — [Substack comments](https://3blue1brown.substack.com/p/opium/comments)
- SoME judging criteria: motivation, clarity, novelty, memorability. Motivation is defined as "It should be clear to the reader/viewer within the first 30 seconds why they should care." — [SoME1 announcement](https://3blue1brown.com/blog/some1), [SoME1 results](https://www.3blue1brown.com/blog/some1-results)
- SoME2 results video (third-party summary): clear examples and "microscale motivation" help engagement and clarity; memorability means creating memorable moments or questions — [Glasp summary](https://glasp.co/youtube/cDofhN-RJqg)
- SoME1 drew over 1,200 submissions — [MAA](https://maa.org/?p=563)
- Fireship: says he does not write a script for every video even though they sound scripted; relies on a Premiere template and stock asset libraries — [transcript](https://gotranscript.com/public/how-i-built-a-successful-youtube-channel-with-programming-tutorials)
- Cleo Abram: short form is for reaching as many people as possible with science concepts and works as a funnel to long-form; every video starts from a key visual — [Creatorpoint](https://creatorpoint.co/how-to-make-youtube-shorts-content-cleo-abrams/), [Podwise](https://beta.podwise.ai/dashboard/episodes/2284662)
- Tom Edgar (MVP) frames source attribution as a deliberate practice that distinguishes his channel — [channel community mirror](https://invidious.cleberg.net/channel/UCT9Fyqn0izh-wX-wDzKBwAA/community)

### Inferences
- Sanderson's position implies a division of labour a pipeline can copy: Shorts carry the question (hook, statement, one concrete instance), long-form carries the explanation. A Short that tries to hold a full derivation is working against the view of the most successful math animator.
- SoME's "why should I care within 30 seconds" compresses to roughly the first sentence in a 45-second Short.
- Fireship's "unscripted" claim conflicts with how tightly the videos are edited; treat it as "no formal script document", not as improvisation (my reading, not sourced).

### Gaps
- No statements found from Zach Star, Andy Math, Michael Stevens, Derek Muller, Kurzgesagt, Presh Talwalkar or bprp specifically about Shorts.
- No Reddit AMA content surfaced. Grant Sanderson's Q&A videos and podcast appearances were listed in results but their contents on Shorts were not retrievable.
- Sanderson's and Abram's quotes are relayed via search summaries; verify wording at the source.

## What separates well-performing educational Shorts, and which topics and framings get the most views?

### Takeaway
Within the one dataset I could verify (3Blue1Brown's 2026 puzzle Shorts), concrete, tangible framings outperform abstract ones by about 2 to 3 times; beyond that, the available "what works" evidence is vendor blog advice with conflicting numbers and should be treated as weak.

### Cited Findings
- Same channel, same format, same year: "lattice bacteria" 838.7K, "ladybug clock" 757.1K, "Phone Number" 742K and "jumping pegs" 569.9K, versus "subset sum" 433.6K, "64 sugar cubes" 349.3K and "Sphere Intersection" 286K (the last was only ~9 days old at snapshot) — [prepublish.ai](https://prepublish.ai/youtube-channel/3blue1brown)
- The solution video for the bacteria puzzle (1.2M) out-viewed its own statement Short (838.7K) — [prepublish.ai](https://prepublish.ai/youtube-channel/3blue1brown)
- Andy Math's breakout was a Desmos-based Short; his recurring format is the geometry area puzzle — [fandom wiki](https://youtube.fandom.com/wiki/Andy_Math)
- A small Manim creator's first proof video passed 1,000 views in three days with under 60 subscribers; the paper attributes this to structure: problem introduced as a question, key formulas listed up front, final result boxed at the end; viewer feedback informed colour contrast, font size and timing choices — [arXiv 2510.01187](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.01187v1)
- Vendor guidance (not peer-reviewed, numbers conflict): the first 3 seconds account for a claimed 50 to 65% of drop-off; educational Shorts can run 35 to 45 s if the payoff is clear; loops let retention exceed 100% so endings should feed the opening; captions are recommended for narration-heavy Shorts because many viewers watch muted; no logo or greeting intros — [prepublish.ai guide](https://prepublish.ai/guides/youtube-shorts-retention), [OpusClip](https://www.opus.pro/blog/ideal-youtube-shorts-length-format-retention), [shortgenius](https://shortgenius.com/blog/youtube-shorts-best-practices), [socialync](https://www.socialync.io/blog/short-form-video-structure-guide-2026)
- YouTube changed how Shorts views are counted, producing a step up in reported views; comparisons across that change are unreliable — [vidIQ video summary](https://rosetta.to/u/vidiq/this-is-what-happens-when-youtube-changes-view-counts)

### Inferences
- The higher-viewed 3Blue1Brown puzzles name a living or everyday thing with motion (bacteria, ladybug, phone number, pegs); the lower ones name a mathematical object (subset sum, sphere intersection). Seven data points with different ages is suggestive, not conclusive.
- Solution content can out-perform the question, so a paired "puzzle Short, then solution Short/long video" series is a reasonable test, despite Sanderson's own move away from solution Shorts.
- Framings with the strongest support across the cited channels: puzzle with a game-like rule set (3Blue1Brown), find-the-area geometry challenge (Andy Math), wordless visual proof of a familiar identity (MVP), "X in 100 seconds" definition of one named thing (Fireship), practical curiosity question (Kurzgesagt's sandwich).

### Gaps
- No rigorous cross-channel dataset of educational Shorts performance by topic or framing was found. Claims about paradoxes, "why does X work" and real-world hooks out-performing are not supported by anything I could cite.
- No retention curves from any of these creators are public as far as the search showed.

## Which animation-only (faceless) educational Shorts channels succeed, and how do they feel human and authored?

### Takeaway
The verified faceless successes are 3Blue1Brown (voice-led, same ManimGL toolchain as this project) and Mathematical Visual Proofs (no voice at all); both establish authorship through a consistent visual identity, a named human behind the channel, and, for MVP, scholarly crediting of each proof. I found no documented case study of an anonymous Manim Shorts channel reaching large scale.

### Cited Findings

### Inferences
- Sourced "authored" signals a pipeline can adopt: (1) a fixed title template and series cadence (3Blue1Brown's "The X puzzle", monthly); (2) crediting where each proof or puzzle comes from in the description (MVP, MoMath/Winkler); (3) a recurring verbal signature (Andy Math's "How exciting", Fireship's sign-off); (4) one stable palette and typography; (5) linking each Short to a longer treatment.
- **[prior knowledge, unverified this session]** Grant Sanderson's narration reads as authored because it uses first person and admits taste ("what I love about this..."), and because animation timing follows the sentence rather than the reverse; synthetic narration that lacks opinion and has uniform pacing is the usual tell of a generated channel.
- With text-to-Manim tools widely available, visuals alone will not differentiate; topic selection, attribution and a consistent voice carry more of the authorship.
